# The evidence, not the claim — why CSOAI publishes artifacts, not conclusions

Most organizations publish conclusions. CSOAI publishes evidence. That's the difference between marketing and measurement — and why it matters for regulators and buyers.

## What's the difference

**A conclusion** is: "X is safe." "X is compliant." "X is better than Y."

**An artifact** is: "Here is what X did on this test condition, signed and verifiable."

Conclusions are claims. Artifacts are evidence.

## Why publishing conclusions is dangerous

When an organization publishes a conclusion:

1. **The conclusion must be defended.** Anyone who disagrees can challenge it.
2. **The conclusion ages.** What was true yesterday may not be today.
3. **The conclusion can be wrong.** Errors become embarrassing retractions.
4. **The conclusion is the product.** The publisher is selling judgment.

When the conclusion is wrong, the publisher's reputation is at stake. So they have incentive to defend the conclusion even when the evidence shifts.

This is what Goodhart's Law looks like in practice: the measure becomes the target, and the target gets gamed.

## Why publishing artifacts is honest

When an organization publishes an artifact:

1. **The artifact stands.** It says what it said at the time it was signed.
2. **The artifact can be corrected.** Superseded by a new artifact; the old one remains.
3. **The artifact is verifiable.** Anyone can re-run the test.
4. **The artifact is the product.** The publisher is selling measurement.

When the measurement changes, the publisher publishes a new measurement. The old one is still there. The corrections ledger records the change.

This is what Goodhart's Law **doesn't** look like: the measure is the measure. It doesn't become the target.

## CSOAI's practice

CSOAI's public surface:

- **Publishes artifacts:** signed cards, public root tree, corrections feed, public verifier, methodology, source code
- **Does NOT publish conclusions:** no "X is safe" claims, no "X is compliant" claims, no "X is better than Y" claims

When someone asks "is this AI safe?", CSOAI's response is "what do you mean by safe? Here is what we measured; you decide."

This is the same posture as a thermometer manufacturer. A thermometer tells you the temperature. It doesn't tell you whether the temperature is "good" or "bad."

## What regulators want

Regulators don't want organizations to make safety claims. They want organizations to produce **evidence** that the regulator can use to make claims.

CSOAI's signed receipts are exactly what regulators need:

- Verifiable
- Versioned
- Timestamped
- Correctable
- Public

A regulator can cite a CSOAI receipt in their own decision-making without trusting CSOAI's marketing.

## What buyers want

Buyers want to know if an AI is safe to deploy. CSOAI cannot tell them that — CSOAI doesn't have the deployment context.

But CSOAI can tell them what the AI does on a specific test. The buyer takes that evidence, applies their own risk tolerance, and makes their own decision.

That's not a cop-out. That's the **honest** answer.

## The bottom line

Evidence, not conclusions. Artifacts, not claims. Let the buyer, the regulator, the journalist interpret.
